# Living Memory Landing Visual Direction V2

**Status:** current visual direction for the next landing-page exploration  
**Date:** 2026-08-11  
**Supersedes:** `LIVING_MEMORY_LANDING_VISUAL_DIRECTION_V1.md` for density, type scale, section spacing, proof treatment, and CTA rhythm

## 1. Design objective

The first rendering proved the information architecture. The second rendering improved clarity but still moved too quickly and compressed proof and conversion opportunities.

The next rendering must prove **emotional pacing and readability**.

Target feeling:

**large editorial family photography + premium archive + calm guided remembering + clear conversion logic**

The page should feel understandable to an older reader without feeling dated to her adult daughter.

## 2. Audience readability hypothesis

Initial commercial design hypothesis:

- primary purchaser/initiator: Baby Boomer women;
- secondary purchaser/initiator: their adult daughters;
- family members of other ages remain fully supported.

Implications:

- larger body type;
- strong contrast;
- fewer simultaneous choices;
- obvious buttons;
- generous line spacing;
- less tiny metadata;
- no hover-dependent understanding;
- no information-dense dashboard look;
- preserve warmth and sophistication rather than using an “accessible senior” stereotype.

## 3. Page rhythm

The page should behave like chapters in a conversation.

### Rule

**One emotional or cognitive job per major section.**

A section may be visually large even when its copy is short.

Use negative space as part of the storytelling.

Suggested visual cadence:

1. light/cream hero;
2. immersive dark or photo-led Magic Moment;
3. light missing-story problem;
4. calm full-width free-offer break;
5. editorial proof section;
6. light mechanism/ease;
7. focused Muse section;
8. free-offer break;
9. large Memory Circle story section;
10. quiet privacy section;
11. spacious Moment → Chapter → Life → Family progression;
12. editorial memorial-belief section;
13. free-offer bridge;
14. pricing;
15. trust/FAQ;
16. final photograph CTA.

Alternating surfaces should create orientation, not decoration.

## 4. Typography

Typography is a product feature for this audience.

### Desktop target

- H1: 56–72 px editorial serif;
- H2: 38–52 px;
- H3: 26–32 px;
- body: 18–20 px;
- supporting metadata: rarely below 16 px;
- buttons: 17–19 px where practical;
- line-height: generous, approximately 1.5–1.7 for body copy.

### Mobile target

- H1: 40–48 px depending on wrapping;
- H2: 30–38 px;
- body: about 18 px;
- supporting copy: 16–18 px;
- minimum control height: 48 px.

Do not shrink type to fit a composition. Make the composition larger.

## 5. Hero

Hero should approach full-screen height on desktop without forcing all content above the fold.

Left or overlaid editorial copy:

> **A photograph preserves the moment.**  
> **Give it its voice.**

One short supporting paragraph.

Primary CTA.

Secondary demo CTA.

One concise trust line.

Right/visual side: one large family photograph with tactile photographic presence.

Do not show the entire Photo → Voice → Muse → Living Memory pipeline in the hero.

## 6. Magic Moment

This is the first major product demonstration and should occupy substantial vertical space.

Use one large image with a calm audio player.

Before playback:

**The photograph**

After playback:

- waveform;
- transcript excerpt;
- 2–3 context chips;
- **Living Memory** label.

The transformation should feel discovered, not diagrammed.

## 7. First free-offer break

Immediately after the demo, use a generous full-width section with minimal competing content.

Headline:

**Try it with one photograph.**

CTA:

**Create One Living Memory Free**

Visual options:

- one hand holding a photograph;
- one simple record button;
- subtle arrow to completed Living Memory;
- no pricing cards nearby.

This should feel like an invitation to participate in what the visitor just saw.

## 8. Missing-story problem

Use one photograph at large scale.

Float or position only 3–4 questions around it.

Avoid six tiny question labels or a dense checklist.

The visual should create the sensation of “I have photographs like this.”

## 9. Proof section

Proof is a full editorial section.

Headline:

**Why a Living Memory makes sense.**

Structure:

### Demonstration proof

Large split visual:

**Photo alone** | **Photo + authentic voice + context**

### Premise proof

Three large stacked or wide blocks, not tiny cards:

1. Visual cues can influence autobiographical remembering.
2. A voice carries speech, identity, and affective information.
3. People often remember through conversation and different perspectives.

Each block gets:

- a simple human illustration/photo detail;
- 1–2 sentence explanation;
- visible source/footnote affordance;
- enough whitespace to read without effort.

### Future product proof

Reserve a clearly labeled area that can later become real customer stories. Until those exist, do not imitate testimonial styling.

Follow proof with another calm free CTA.

## 10. Mechanism and ease

Separate these into two visual beats if necessary.

### Mechanism

Large sequence:

**Photo → Voice → Muse → Living Memory**

### Ease

Four roomy steps:

1. Pick a photograph.
2. Tell the story.
3. Muse helps.
4. Hear your Living Memory.

Icons support copy but do not replace readable text.

## 11. Muse

Use a realistic mobile interaction visual and only four first-level benefits.

Headline:

**You remember. Muse helps.**

Trust callout receives its own panel:

> **Muse never invents your memory.**

Keep sparkles/icons restrained.

Follow with a free CTA aimed at technology/effort anxiety.

## 12. Memory Circle

Memory Circle should become one of the largest sections after the Magic Moment.

Headline:

**Some stories are better remembered together.**

Hero composition:

- photograph or Living Memory remains visually central;
- people gathered physically around it;
- optional remote participants integrated naturally;
- avoid a grid of business-call headshots;
- one visible active storyteller;
- subtle recording/consent state;
- recollections appear as attributed layers around the memory.

Show the idea of anywhere:

**At the kitchen table. Across the country. Across generations.**

The section should visually communicate that the product can preserve the *telling moment itself*—the questions, recognition, laughter, corrections, and emotion—when participants consent.

## 13. Privacy

Make privacy visually quiet.

Large lock or archive icon.

Headline:

**Private first. Shared when you choose.**

Then four large choices:

Private / Family / Friend / Facebook

Do not turn privacy into a technical permissions matrix on the landing page.

## 14. Moment → Chapter → Life → Family

Give each stage enough width and image scale to feel meaningful.

Avoid a tiny four-icon row.

Possible desktop treatment: four large editorial panels revealed horizontally or vertically.

Possible mobile treatment: one stage per screen-width card with obvious progression.

No price yet.

## 15. Memorial belief

Use a quiet, almost magazine-like composition.

One portrait/photo.

Large copy:

> **We've always found ways to remember the people we love.**

Then:

> A monument can preserve a name and dates. A Living Memory can preserve more of the life between them.

Give this line generous space:

> **Preserve more of the life between the dates.**

No gravestone imagery is necessary. The idea is stronger than literal death imagery.

## 16. Pre-pricing free bridge

Before pricing, remind the visitor that no paid decision is required to prove the mechanism.

Headline:

**Experience one Living Memory first.**

Primary CTA: **Create Your First Living Memory Free**

Secondary: **Compare Ways to Preserve More**

This bridge should reduce price shock and preserve the product-led funnel.

## 17. Pricing

Pricing should feel spacious and comprehensible.

Desktop: three large cards are acceptable only if the type remains comfortably readable.

Mobile: stack cards.

Each card initially shows:

- outcome;
- price;
- 4–5 strongest benefits;
- CTA;
- expandable **See everything included**.

Do not expose 10–15 tiny bullets by default.

Life should stand out because its outcome and Memory Circle make sense, not because of a fabricated popularity badge.

## 18. Free CTA visual rhythm

Free CTAs may appear at:

1. hero;
2. post-demo full section;
3. post-proof;
4. post-Muse/ease;
5. pre-pricing;
6. final section.

They should not all look identical.

Alternate treatments:

- full primary button;
- editorial invitation band;
- photo + CTA card;
- simple text/arrow action;
- final high-emotion CTA.

Every treatment represents the same entitlement: **one complete Living Memory free**.

## 19. Proof integrity in visual design

Premise research must visually look like research/evidence.

Customer proof must visually look like customer proof only after it exists.

Do not use:

- invented headshots with quote marks as though they were customers;
- fake star ratings;
- fake family counts;
- fake publication logos;
- “trusted by thousands” placeholders.

A synthetic photograph may illustrate the product, but it must not be captioned as a customer testimonial.

## 20. Acceptance test for rendering 3

Rendering 3 passes only if a reviewer can say yes to all of these:

- Is the page easier to read than rendering 2?
- Does each section have a single dominant idea?
- Is the first free Living Memory impossible to miss without feeling pushed?
- Does the free invitation reappear after belief increases?
- Is proof visibly present and substantive?
- Is proof honest about what research versus customers prove?
- Does Memory Circle feel like a family remembrance experience from anywhere?
- Can an older reader comfortably read the body copy without zooming?
- Does the page feel premium rather than “senior-oriented”?
- Does the daughter audience see a product she could help a parent start?
- Is the memorial belief moving but not morbid?
- Does pricing arrive late enough?
- Does the page remain software-first and human-memory-first rather than AI-first?
